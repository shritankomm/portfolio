## The problem

Most capable robotic arms are either industrial units costing tens of thousands of dollars, or hobby kits with two or three degrees of freedom that can't approximate real human-arm motion. There wasn't an accessible, fully open-source middle ground: a 7-DOF arm — enough to match a human shoulder-elbow-wrist configuration — that a maker with an FDM printer and a few hundred dollars could actually build, with a real software stack and documentation, not just a parts list.

## The build

The 7-DOF FDM Open Arm is a full-scale humanoid robotic arm, structurally 3D-printed (FDM) end to end. It's driven by 16 Feetech smart servos and controlled by a Raspberry Pi 4 running the full ROS 2 stack onboard, with onboard vision for perception rather than offloading to a desktop. The arm supports two teleoperation modes — a real-time bilateral mirror setup and VR teleoperation — and ships with a full long-form YouTube build guide so anyone can reproduce it.

## The approach

The software stack is ROS 2 end to end. Motion planning runs through MoveIt 2, using `pick_ik` as the inverse kinematics solver — chosen over the default KDL solver because its cost-function approach handles the arm's redundant 7th degree of freedom (null-space motion) far better, letting the arm reposition its elbow without moving the end effector. Everything is validated in simulation before touching hardware, which caught several joint-limit and self-collision issues that would have snapped a printed bracket on the real arm.

Open-sourcing it properly — CAD, BOM, ROS 2 packages, and a full build guide — was as much the point as the hardware itself, which is also why it was built with Hack Club funding: to make sure a genuinely capable arm stays reproducible by other makers, not just a one-off build.

## The result

The arm holds and moves through its full 7-DOF range under both teleoperation modes, shown in the linked video and full build guide. It was recognized at a buildathon, and the complete build — CAD, BOM, and ROS 2 packages — is public on GitHub so other makers can reproduce it for under $750 in parts.
